I had mixed feelings about my TTRPG experiment this past week, “Blood Spores.” I think I had too many design epiphanies too late in the process. I would have really liked to dive more into the GM-less, procedural generation side of things, but I only really thought about that the day before it was due. As such, the version I turned in was essentially a few interesting ideas that aren’t really held together by anything substantial. It would have been nice to spend more time iterating so I could come to those conclusions prior to playtesting - but, as a counterpoint, that’s exactly the point of playtesting these experiments anyways. I don’t know if I feel strongly enough about the premise to make it my final project, but at least now I have more ammo to work with if I do.

Playtesting in class is a great experience for many reasons, but primarily because it makes me want to be more creative with my ideas. When everyone else in the class comes in with new and inventive stuff, it makes me feel like I have to step up my own game. I also think I need to be less of a perfectionist/completionist, in the sense that I have to be more willing to come in with something like Blood Spores that’s a little half-baked, because it seems like everyone in the class always has the exact right ideas for how to make it into a full thing. I really enjoyed seeing people’s solo/solo journalling games this time around specifically - I thought Manuel’s was so cool in the way it dealt with memory, and the way you journal out a whole life instead of just one adventure or “campaign.”

Playing The Quiet Year was cool, although I think map-making games are not my favorite genre (genre? slippery word) of TTRPG. The thing I liked most about The Quiet Year was its restraint, particularly when it came to the prompts and the limited world-building. The best writing in TTRPGs, I’ve found, is succinct and evocative. “The Frost Shepherds arrive… the game is over” is such good writing. I love a symbolically abrupt ending. 

Mechanically (a word which I need to find some good synonyms for ASAP), I enjoyed the way the prompts grew more involved or had higher stakes as the seasons progressed. It created a very nice learning curve and gradual tutorialization of the game without making it obvious. Every TTRPG has some amount of time where the players are playing, but they haven’t fully bought into it yet (and that time can vary depending on both the game and the players). The Quiet Year, in a way, makes that time part of the game itself. Also, the dice counting down just feels good, and it’s a neat way to add a physical element to the space and make the game feel more three-dimensional, both literally and figuratively.

I think map-making games are cool, but one quality of TTRPGs that I like is the way that everyone can have a completely different mental image of the game in their heads. Every playthrough will look different in people’s imagination, but I felt when playing The Quiet Year that it was actually more difficult for me to abstract out from a drawing than to abstract out from a description. If we had class today I’d be curious to hear if that’s how other people feel as well, because if that’s a universal experience, then everyone kind of has the same mental image of the game, which is less interesting to me. It may just be the specific way I interact with map-making games, though.
