I took a bit of a swing with my experiment this past week, the choose-your-own-adventure-type gamebook. From an analytical perspective, it was extremely cool to think about how to make roleplaying systems work in such a restrictive medium, and how the mechanics of writing change significantly for a CYOA book. That said, I don’t think the end product was that compelling, and probably won’t be my final project. Among other things, I think it was a bit of a misstep to go with the space theme, since I found myself not that inspired to actually write out any long story branches, but at the same time, it was fun to try and recapture/improve upon the book that I was inexplicably intrigued by when I was eight.

It’s weird that the Fighting Fantasy gamebooks are grouped in with TTRPGs because they really are so limited in their “roleplay” abilities. To me, it parallels video game RPGs, where the extent of the roleplaying is simple narrative choices and some different approaches to or numbers during combat. Considering it mostly consists of actual story writing, it’s a surprisingly number-y format with not a lot of narrative flexibility (due to lack of a GM or any personal creative license). It’s an interesting conundrum to try and work around, but one that I wasn’t really able to work around in 2 weeks, aside from cosmetic things like having a catchphrase for your character.

There were a few map-making games in class this past week, which I found interesting. I’m waffling on whether to do a map-making game for my own experiment four. I did really like playing The Quiet Year, but I wonder if I’d have trouble coming up with enough of a twist that it’s not just The Quiet Year again. Josh’s game involving memory loss was an interesting twist, so maybe some sort of mechanic that involves distorting existing drawings. I can actually make use of the eraser end of the pencil.

I wish we had more time in class to talk about D&D. Perhaps I was giving it an overly charitable read because I like his work, but I didn’t find the Brennan Lee Mulligan “oven” quote to be that controversial. I went and read this essay about it, which helped me see why people were so upset (maybe not why they were so upset, but what they were saying when they were upset, at least). I think I play D&D with a similar mindset to Mulligan in the sense that I feel no sense of urgency about adhering to the rules, that they exist as a framing device for telling a story. I feel this way about pretty much all TTRPGs, and I’m totally willing to be academically wrong about that. 

What I suspect he was trying to get at with his specific analogy (again, maybe being too charitable) is that the rules of D&D may be combat-oriented, but the game itself is more than just its rules. The game is a combination of the rules and what you and all the other players bring to the table, i.e. the food. That’s pretty much how I feel as well. In addition, some of that might be due to D&D’s extensive history and how the game has evolved over time. I’m sure that when it originated, people were spending a lot more time on the combat (the war games DNA of it all) and less time on telling stories. And while the rules have changed a lot, often for the better, the game (holistically) has changed a lot as well. It’s become a sort of mythic version of itself, “the greatest role-playing game ever” or whatever Hasbro’s tagline was. People aren’t saying it’s the greatest game ever because the armor class system is really good, or because they love moving figurines around on tiles, they’re saying that because they remember that one time their friend betrayed them. Or more likely, they’re remembering that Dimension 20 clip they saw where Brennan Lee Mulligan did a monologue. The mythic, collective consciousness version of D&D is - similar to Mulligan’s interpretation - not very combat oriented. It's also, for better or for worse, not very specific anymore.

I might be talking complete nonsense right now. Not an academic, these were just my immediate thoughts that I didn’t get to voice during the shortened class discussion. To be fair, though, most people aren’t saying D&D the greatest game ever at all. Hasbro, much like both the internet at large and Brennan Lee Mulligan doing a monologue, needs to relax.
